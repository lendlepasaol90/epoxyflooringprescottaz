Hangar and warehouse floor coatings

Prescott has an active aviation community and a growing number of warehouses, shops and maintenance buildings. Their floors must resist fuels and fluids, stay free of dust and debris, and reflect light for close work.

Request an estimate(928) 555-0186

A bright, sealed hangar floor

Prescott Regional Airport supports a busy mix of private owners, flight training, maintenance shops and charter operations, and the hangars around it hold aircraft worth a great deal. Bare concrete is a poor match for that environment. It sheds dust that can become foreign-object debris, absorbs fuel and hydraulic fluid, hides leaks and darkens the space.

The same issues, in different forms, affect warehouses, fabrication shops and maintenance buildings across the Prescott area. A properly specified coating addresses them: it seals the concrete, brightens the building, resists the chemicals in use and makes the floor easy to keep clean. This page explains our approach.

What a hangar floor must do

A good hangar floor does several jobs at once.

Resist aviation fluids. Jet fuel, avgas, oils and hydraulic fluids are spilled from time to time. Some aircraft use phosphate-ester hydraulic fluids that attack ordinary coatings, so the topcoat must be chosen with the fleet in mind.

Reveal leaks. A light-colored floor makes drips and stains immediately visible beneath an aircraft, which helps owners and mechanics catch problems early.

Reflect light. Bright floors bounce light up under wings and into wheel wells, making inspection and maintenance easier.

Stay clean. A sealed surface does not shed dust or grit that could be drawn into engines or propellers.

Bear the load. Tugs, tow bars, jacks and maintenance stands all concentrate weight on small areas.

Systems for hangars and industrial buildings

We match the system to the activity, the fluids and the temperature of the building.

Hangar system

Ground and repaired slab, resilient base, light-colored build coat and a fluid-resistant topcoat with modest texture.

High-build epoxy

Joints, cracks and transitions

In hangars and warehouses, saw-cut joints and cracks are where floors deteriorate first. Wheels crossing open joints chip their edges, and fluids collect in them. Before coating, we clean out joints, rebuild damaged edges and fill them with semi-rigid material suited to the traffic. The threshold at the hangar door, where interior and exterior concrete meet and temperatures swing most, receives particular attention, as do floor drains and trench grates.

Markings, zones and safety

Floor markings bring order to a busy building. We lay out and coat:

  • Aircraft parking positions and centerlines
  • Walkways and keep-clear zones around equipment
  • Forklift lanes and staging areas in warehouses
  • Fire equipment, panels and exits
  • Color-coded work and storage zones

Because these markings form part of the coating system rather than paint on bare concrete, they keep their definition through years of traffic and cleaning.

Temperature and timing in Prescott

Many hangars and industrial buildings in the area are unheated or only partly heated. In winter, slab temperatures can approach freezing; in summer, buildings warm considerably. Both affect how coatings are applied and how they perform.

We schedule winter work for heated spaces or bring in temporary heat, choose cold-tolerant products where needed and favor flexible base coats in buildings with large temperature swings. Large doors also admit snow, deicer and dust, so threshold areas are built with extra durability and texture.

Specialist work for specialist buildings

Hangar and industrial floors require heavy grinding equipment, sound knowledge of how coatings respond to specific chemicals and loads, and careful planning around operations. A failure in an occupied hangar or warehouse costs far more than the floor. We take the time to specify correctly and to work cleanly around aircraft, stock and staff.

Choosing colors for hangars and warehouses

Light greys and off-whites are the usual choice for hangar floors because they reflect the most light and reveal leaks clearly. Warehouses often favor a light grey field with safety yellow lanes. Darker colors absorb light and show the pale dust that blows in through large doors. We recommend a limited palette, with accent colors reserved for markings so that they remain meaningful.

Costs and maintenance

Pricing reflects the condition of the slab, the thickness and type of system, the extent of joint repair, access around aircraft or racking, staging and markings. Each element is itemized so investment can be directed where it matters most.

Maintenance is straightforward: regular sweeping or scrubbing with neutral cleaners, prompt cleanup of spills and periodic inspection of high-wear areas. Renewing the topcoat in a worn area is far less disruptive than repairing a failed floor.

Dust, debris and engine safety

In aviation, loose material on the floor is more than untidy. Grit and concrete dust can be drawn into engines and propellers or blown about during run-ups and taxiing near hangar doors. Bare concrete constantly sheds fine particles as tires and equipment wear its surface. Sealing the floor removes that source, and a light, smooth color makes any debris easy to see and sweep up before it becomes a problem.

Fabrication and maintenance shops

Welding, grinding and machining shops are punishing environments for floors: sparks, dropped stock, cutting fluids and heavy fixtures. A high-build or polyurea system resists impact and chemicals far better than bare concrete, and a light color improves visibility at the bench. Where hot work is routine, we discuss realistic expectations, since sparks can mark any coating, and recommend weld mats in the hottest areas.

Warehouse aisles and racking

Warehouses rarely need the same system everywhere. Drive aisles and turning areas carry the most abrasion and benefit from a thicker build, while the floor beneath racking may only need sealing. Staging and dock areas take snowmelt and grit through winter. We tailor the specification zone by zone so the budget goes where the wear is.

Hangar doors and thresholds

The line beneath a hangar door is one of the most demanding parts of the floor. It sees aircraft tires, tugs, snow and rain blown in when the door is open and the greatest temperature swings in the building. We detail this area carefully: repairing the edge of the slab, treating the joint with flexible material and carrying the coating to a clean termination point. A little extra texture here helps footing when the threshold is wet.

Questions about hangar and warehouse floors

Will the coating resist aircraft hydraulic fluid?

It depends on the fluid. Some phosphate-ester hydraulic fluids attack ordinary coatings, so we specify topcoats rated for them where they are used. Tell us about your aircraft and maintenance activity at the survey.

How long must the hangar be empty?

Typically a few days for a single hangar, depending on size, repairs and the system. Rapid-curing topcoats shorten the time before aircraft can return.

Can you coat an unheated hangar in winter?

Sometimes, with temporary heat and cold-tolerant products. Otherwise we recommend a milder period.

Do you mark aircraft parking positions?

Yes. Parking positions, centerlines and walkways can be incorporated into the coating.

Can you work around warehouse racking?

Yes. Aisles and open areas can be coated without moving racking, or full coverage can be achieved in stages.

Is a sealer sufficient for a storage building?

For light traffic and dust control alone, a densifier and sealer may suffice. Fluids, forklifts and markings generally call for a full system.

Can you coat a T-hangar while my aircraft is away for maintenance?

That is often an ideal opportunity. Let us know the dates, and we will plan the work to be complete and cured before the aircraft returns.

Can the coating continue onto the apron outside?

Interior systems are not intended for full exterior exposure. The apron is better served by a penetrating sealer, with a neat transition at the door line.

Will a light floor show tire marks?

Some marks appear over time, particularly from tugs. They clean off a sealed floor far more easily than from bare concrete, and a suitable topcoat resists them.
