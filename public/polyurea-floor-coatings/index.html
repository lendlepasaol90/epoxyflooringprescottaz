Polyurea coatings for older slabs and hard-working buildings

Old concrete, unheated buildings and heavy equipment all ask more of a floor coating than rigid materials can comfortably give. Polyurea flexes, absorbs impact and holds fast, which is why we rely on it for the most demanding floors.

Flexible base under a working floor

Concrete moves more than most people realize. In Prescott, a detached garage or barn can drop well below freezing at night and warm considerably in the afternoon sun. Older slabs have developed networks of fine cracks over the decades. Additions poured years apart meet at joints that open and close with the seasons. And workshops, barns and equipment buildings take impacts that would chip ordinary coatings.

Rigid coatings bridging those cracks and joints tend to fail along them. Polyurea behaves differently: it cures tough yet flexible, stretching slightly with the concrete and spreading impact rather than fracturing. This page describes when we recommend it and how we install it.

Movement in Prescott concrete

Three factors account for most of the movement we see in local slabs.

Temperature. Unheated garages, barns and hangars experience large daily swings for much of the year. Concrete expands and contracts with every cycle.

Age. Older slabs have usually developed shrinkage cracks and, in some cases, joints between sections poured at different times. These are normal, but they move.

Ground. Soils across the area vary, from decomposed granite to clay, and some shift slightly with moisture after snowmelt and monsoon rains.

None of this indicates a faulty slab. It simply means the coating should be capable of accommodating some movement.

Barns, shops and rural outbuildings

Properties in Williamson Valley, Skull Valley, Chino Valley and the surrounding countryside often include barns, shops and equipment buildings with concrete floors that see genuine work. Tractors, trailers, livestock equipment and vehicles come and go; tools and parts are dropped; and the buildings are rarely heated.

A polyurea base suits these conditions particularly well. It tolerates cold and movement, absorbs impact and provides a strong foundation for a textured topcoat that gives secure footing when the floor is wet or muddy. Where budget is a consideration, we can coat the working areas fully and seal storage bays more simply.

How it is installed

  1. Survey the slab

    Cracks, joints and any signs of structural movement are recorded.

  2. Grind

    Diamond grinding opens the surface for a deep bond.

  3. Treat cracks and joints

    Moving cracks receive flexible filler; stable damage receives rigid repair.

  4. Polyurea base

    Applied in sections, pigmented or with a flake broadcast.

  5. Topcoat

    A protective, textured topcoat suited to the building’s use.

Suited to the Prescott area

Between freezing winters, monsoon summers and slabs ranging from new to nearly a century old, Prescott presents a wide variety of concrete. A flexible base is often our recommendation for detached garages, carriage houses, barns, hangars and any floor with a history of cracking or chipping.

We are also clear about its limits. If a slab is actively settling or heaving, cracks may reappear through any coating, and we will point out what we observe before you commit to anything.

A material for professionals

Polyurea sets quickly and must be mixed precisely, applied to freshly ground concrete and spread swiftly and evenly. Deciding which cracks require flexible treatment, which can be repaired rigidly and which warrant further investigation is a matter of judgment developed over many projects.

Carriage houses and mixed-age slabs

Many older properties near downtown have carriage houses or garages that have been extended over the years, so the floor may consist of two or three slabs poured decades apart. The joints between them often move at different rates. A rigid coating laid across those joints tends to crack along them. With a polyurea base and flexible joint treatment, the floor can accommodate that movement while presenting a single, finished surface.

Value and upkeep

Polyurea costs somewhat more than standard epoxy, but preparation and labor make up most of a project's price, so the difference overall is usually modest. For unheated buildings, older slabs and heavily used floors, the added resilience generally repays itself.

Care is straightforward: regular sweeping, clearing meltwater in winter and occasional washing with a neutral cleaner. Place pads beneath jack stands and kickstands. The topcoat can be renewed in future without disturbing the base.

Hangar tugs and maintenance stands

In hangars, the heaviest loads often come not from the aircraft's tires but from tugs, tow bars, jacks and maintenance stands, which concentrate weight on small steel feet. A polyurea base absorbs those loads well and resists the chipping that rigid coatings can suffer around jacking points. Combined with a fluid-resistant topcoat, it forms a durable foundation for an aviation floor.

Agricultural and equestrian buildings

Barns, tack rooms, wash bays and equipment sheds on ranch properties around Prescott face moisture, manure, feed, hoof traffic and heavy machinery. A polyurea base beneath a well-textured topcoat stands up to that combination better than most alternatives, cleans with a hose and resists the chemicals in common cleaners and disinfectants. Texture is kept generous in wash bays and walkways where footing matters most, for people and animals alike.

Questions about polyurea

How does polyurea differ from polyaspartic?

Polyaspartic is a member of the polyurea family formulated for topcoats, with more working time and light stability. When we refer to polyurea, we generally mean the flexible base layer.

Will cracks reappear?

Ordinary hairline cracks and joint movement are accommodated far better by a flexible base. Cracks caused by active settlement can still show through any coating.

Can a barn floor be coated?

Yes, provided it is concrete. Polyurea is particularly well suited to unheated agricultural buildings.

How cold may it be during installation?

It is more forgiving of a chilly morning than epoxy, though not without limit. If the slab is too cold on the day, we warm the building first or move the date.

When can the floor be used?

The base cures quickly; the topcoat usually determines timing, typically within a day or two.

Can polyurea be colored?

Yes. It can be pigmented or used beneath decorative flakes.

Is polyurea suitable for a hangar?

Often, as a resilient base beneath a topcoat chosen for aviation fluids. See our hangar and warehouse page.

Is polyurea worthwhile in a heated attached garage?

Not always. If the slab is sound and the garage is heated, an epoxy base may serve just as well at a slightly lower cost. We advise according to the floor.

Can polyurea go in a horse wash bay?

Yes, with a coarse, slip-resistant texture and attention to drainage. We discuss the use of the space carefully before specifying the finish.

Can you coat only part of a large barn floor?

Certainly. Many owners coat the working aisles, wash bay and tack room fully and seal storage bays more simply. We price each area separately so you can choose.

How does polyurea cope with manure and feed acids?

With an appropriate topcoat, it resists these well and cleans easily with a hose and a mild cleaner. Prompt rinsing extends the life of any finish.
