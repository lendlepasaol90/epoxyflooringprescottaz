Residential epoxy flooring for basements and living spaces

Prescott has more basements, cellars and lower levels than most Arizona towns, along with studios, casitas and laundry rooms built on concrete. A seamless coated floor can make those rooms cleaner, brighter and far easier to live with.

Request an estimate(928) 555-0186

A light-filled room ready for a new floor

Interior concrete is common in Prescott homes, more so than in much of Arizona. Older houses near the historic center often have full or partial basements. Hillside homes have walk-out lower levels. Many properties include a casita, studio or converted outbuilding on a slab. These rooms frequently end up with tired carpet, cold vinyl or bare concrete that is hard to keep clean.

A coated floor is a practical and attractive answer, provided the work is done with care. Interior concrete must be tested for moisture, prepared without filling the house with dust, and finished in a way that suits the character of the home. This page explains how we approach those projects.

Basements and lower levels

Basements are where moisture matters most. Concrete below grade is in contact with soil that can become damp after snowmelt and monsoon rains. Water vapor moves up through the slab, and if it is trapped beneath a coating, it can cause blistering or loss of adhesion.

That is why every basement project begins with moisture testing. If readings are within acceptable limits, we proceed with the standard system. If they are elevated, we use a moisture-mitigating primer designed to block vapor before the decorative layers are applied. If we see signs of active water, such as staining, efflorescence or damp walls, we will recommend addressing drainage first.

Once properly coated, a basement floor becomes a bright, washable surface suited to a family room, gym, workshop or storage.

Interior finishes

Inside a home, the floor must look deliberate and complement the rooms around it.

Satin solid color

Low sheen in warm greys, birch or soft slate. Quiet and elegant, and forgiving of footprints.

Fine flake

A subtle speckled texture, practical in laundry rooms, mudrooms and studios.

Metallic

Flowing, reflective pigments for a statement basement or family room. See metallic epoxy.

Clear-sealed concrete

For slabs with attractive natural character, a clear system that preserves the concrete look.

How an interior project proceeds

  1. Consultation and testing

    We measure, test moisture and show samples in the room.

  2. Protection

    Walls, stairs and adjacent rooms are protected and the work area sealed off.

  3. Removal and grinding

    Existing flooring and adhesive are removed and the slab ground smooth.

  4. Repairs and priming

    Cracks are filled; a moisture-mitigating primer is applied where needed.

  5. Finish layers

    Color, flake or metallic layers, followed by a protective topcoat.

Interior floors in Prescott homes

Prescott's older homes often have basements with concrete poured long before modern vapor barriers were standard. Hillside homes may have lower levels where groundwater moves after heavy storms. Newer homes typically have drier slabs but may still benefit from testing in rooms against the hillside.

Winters here are cold enough that unheated basements and casitas can have chilly slabs. Coatings need the floor within a certain temperature range to cure correctly, so we keep the space warm during installation.

Large south- and west-facing windows bring strong sunlight at this elevation. In bright rooms, we use UV-stable topcoats to keep colors true.

The value of a careful installer

Indoors, flaws are easily seen: ridges from leftover adhesive, specks of dust in the topcoat, uneven sheen or rough edges at baseboards. Interior work also requires managing dust and odor in an occupied home and planning transitions so doors open freely. We take the time each interior project requires and inspect the finished floor in good light before handing it over.

Choosing colors for older and newer homes

In older homes, warm greys, soft birch and walnut-toned finishes sit comfortably beside painted woodwork and brick. In newer homes, cooler greys and slate tones complement modern cabinetry and stone. If you are uncertain, we can prepare tinted samples to view beside your trim and cabinets before deciding.

Care and long-term upkeep

Dust mop routinely and damp mop with a neutral cleaner as needed. Felt pads beneath furniture prevent scuffs, and mats at exterior doors catch grit. Avoid harsh chemicals and abrasive pads. After many years, the topcoat in busy areas can be lightly abraded and renewed to restore its sheen.

Factors that affect the price

The existing flooring influences cost significantly: carpet is quick to remove, while tile and adhesive take longer. Moisture mitigation, when required, adds a layer. Satin solid colors and fine flake sit at the more economical end; metallic finishes cost more. Smaller rooms may carry a higher cost per square foot because setup time is similar. Each element is itemized in your estimate.

Wine rooms and cellars

Cool, stable basements make natural wine rooms, and a number of Prescott homeowners have converted cellar space for the purpose. A coated floor suits these rooms well: it is seamless, simple to clean when a bottle is dropped and resistant to the occasional red-wine spill that would stain bare concrete or grout permanently. Satin finishes in deep slate or walnut tones give the room a quiet, finished character. Because cellars are below grade, moisture testing is especially important, and a vapor-blocking primer is used whenever readings call for it.

Studios and home workshops

Prescott has a strong community of artists and makers, and many work from a studio at home: a converted garage, a casita or a room off the patio. Paint, clay, resin, sawdust and solvents are all hard on conventional flooring. A coated slab withstands them, can be wiped or scraped clean and does not hold dust the way carpet or bare concrete does. A light, matte color helps with judging color accurately under daylight, and a fine texture keeps footing secure around wet work.

Mudrooms for mountain living

Between snow in winter, monsoon mud in summer and hiking boots year-round, the mudroom is one of the hardest-working spaces in many Prescott homes. A fine flake finish with slip-resistant texture hides grit, cleans with a mop and does not mind melting snow or wet dogs. It also provides a durable, attractive transition between the garage and the rest of the house.

Questions about interior floors

Can my damp basement be coated?

Often, yes, with a moisture-mitigating primer. If there is active water entry, that should be resolved first. We test and inspect before recommending anything.

Will the floor feel cold?

Coated concrete feels similar to tile. Rugs add warmth where you sit, and coated slabs work well with radiant floor heating.

Can you coat over existing tile?

It is not something we recommend. Glazed tile offers a poor grip, and within a season or two the grid of grout joints usually becomes visible through the new surface. Lifting the tile first is more work but produces a floor you will be pleased with.

How long will the room be unavailable?

Most rooms are out of commission for the better part of a working week, though you may walk across in soft shoes the morning after the final coat.

Is there much dust?

We use vacuum-attached grinders and seal off the work area. Some fine dust is unavoidable, but it is kept to a minimum.

Can a coated floor go in a wine room?

Yes. A seamless floor is easy to clean, and spills wipe up without staining.

Can you match my cabinets or trim?

We can come close with standard colors and tints, and we test samples beside your finishes.

Can a basement stairway be coated as well?

Concrete stairs can be coated to match the floor, with a textured finish and attention to the nosings. Wooden stairs are not suitable for these systems.

Is a coated mudroom slippery with snow?

With a textured topcoat, it gives secure footing even when wet. A mat inside the door catches most of the snow before it reaches the floor.
